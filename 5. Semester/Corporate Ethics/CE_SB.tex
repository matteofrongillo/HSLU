Study of morality that focuses on explaining rules and principles that
determine right or wrong.

\subsection{Globalization}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Globalization is a process which diminishes the necessity of a common and shared territorial basis for social, economic, and political activities, processes, and relations (``deterritorialization'')
    \item Globalization makes real-time social, political, economic, and cultural exchanges possible between people and organizations without the need for physical contact
\end{itemize}

Globalization was supported by two main developments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Technology (communication, transportation, digital financial streams)
    \item Policy (national territorial borders have been eroded)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Effects}
\begin{itemize}
    \item European financial crisis $\to$ events, which have local roots, have effects on seemingly disconnected places
    \item Global climate change $\to$ Local causes leads to global problems and global solutions (eg. Kyoto Protocol)
    \item Acts of Terror $\to$ events, people or ideas from faraway places can have effects on people on unconnected locations/situations (eg. security checks in airports)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Ethics in the context of globalization}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Topics like fair trade, food prices, financialstability have set ethical spotlight on globalization process
    \item MNCs (Multi-National Corporations) are at the center of public's criticism on globalization\\$\to$ accused to exploit workers, cause environmental destruction,abuse their economic power
    \item Globalization is the most demanding area for corporation to define and legitimate their ``right and wrong'' behavior
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concepts distinct from globalization}
\subsubsection{Internationalization}
Internationalization is the expansion of activities across national borders while those activities remain anchored in particular countries.

\subsubsection{Westernization}
Westernization is the spread or adoption of Western cultural practices, values, and institutions.

\subsubsection{Homogenisation}
Homogenisation is the process by which cultures, products, or practices become more similar to one another.

\subsection{Relevance of globalization for business ethics}
\subsubsection{Cultural issues}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corporations increasingly engage in overseas markets
    \item Confronted with new, diverse or even contradictory ethical demands
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Legal issues}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Linked to relationship between ethics and law
    \item Transactions lose connection to certain territorial entity
    \item Escape legal control of national governments
    \item Globalization increases demand for business ethics (beyond control of national governments)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Accountability issues}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Some MNCs are as economically powerful as entire countries
    \item How to control MNCs, which have higher revenues than GDPs of entire countries?
    \item Demand for corporate accountability
\end{itemize}

\section{Framing business ethics}
\subsection{Stock corporation}
A corporation is defined in terms of legal status and the ownership
of assets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Legally corporations are independents from those who manage, work, invest in them or receive products or services from them
    \item Corporation have perpetual succession
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Corporation assets owners}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A corporation owns its own assets (eg. factories, equipment)
    \item Shareholders only own share, which entitles them from dividends and some decisions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implications of corporation definition for determining responsibilities}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corporations are regarded as ``artificial persons'' in the eyes of the law\\$\to$ have certain rights and (legal) responsibilities in society
    \item Corporations are ``owned'' by shareholders, but exists independently of them\\$\to$ shareholders are not responbile for debts or damages
    \item Managers and directors have responsibility to protect investment of shareholders
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\k{Can a corporation have social responsibility?}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Only human beings have a moral responsibility for their actions
    \item It is managers' responsibility to act solely in the interests of shareholders (profit oriented)
    \item Social issues and problems are the proper province of the state rather than corporate managers\\$\to$ corporations should not decide what is in society's best interest
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{``The social responsibility of business is to increase its profits''}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corporations can decide/act independent of their members\\
        $\to$ Corporate internal decision structure based on predetermined goals
    \item All corporations have a set of beliefs and values (organizational culture)
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Influence on individual's ethical decision-making and behavior
        \item Corporations present and interact with stakeholders as if they were distinct persons
    \end{itemize}
    \item Corporations have some level of moral responsibility that is more than the responsibility of the individuals constituing the corporation
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why do corporations have and take on social responsibilities?}
\subsubsection{Internal business reasons (``enlightened self-interest'')}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extra and/or satisfied customers/employees $\to$ enhances long-term revenues
    \item Cost reduction due to increased sustainability $\to$ saving energy, waste reduction, less inefficiencies
    \item Manage risks and uncertainty
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Committing to social actions/programmes may forestall legislation
        \item Prevent penalties
    \end{itemize}
    \item Long-term investment which benefits corporation $\to$ Maintaining consent of local communities, employees, governments
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Moral reasons}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corporations cause social problems (externalities)\\
        $\to$ Moral responsibility to deal with them (eg. pollution, resource depletion, community problems)
    \item Corporations should use their power responsibly
    \item Corporations rely on the contribution of a wide set of stakeholders in society, not just shareholders\\
        $\to$ Have a duty to also take into account the interests of stakeholders
\end{itemize}

    \item Stakeholders vary from company to company and for the same company in different situations
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluating business ethics}
\subsection{Ethical theories}
\subsubsection{\k{Normative ethical theories (focus on this session)}}
Attempts to explain the morally correct way of acting

\subsubsection{\k{Descriptive ethical theories (focus on next session)}}
Attempts to describe how ethics decisions are actually made in business

\subsection{Role of ethical theories}
\subsubsection{\k{Ethical absolutism}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Claims that eternal, universally applicable moral principles exist
    \item Right and wrong are objective qualities
    \item Can be rationally determined
    \item (Typically) Non-consequentialist traditional ethical theories
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\k{Ethical relativism}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Claims that morality is context-dependent and subjective
    \item No universal right and wrongs that can be rationally determined
    \item Depends on person making the decision
    \item Depends on culture in which they are located
    \item (Typically) Contemporary ethical theories
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\k{Ethical pluralism}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Middle ground between absolutism and relativism
    \item Accepts different moral backgrounds
    \item Suggests consensus on basic principles/rules in social context
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Classical ethical theories}
\subsubsection{\k{Theory of egoism}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An action is morally right if in a given situation\\
    $\to$ All decision-makers freely decide to pursue their (short-therm) desires or their (long-therm) interest
    \item Justification: humans only have limited insight into the consequences of their actions\\
    $\to$ Only suitable strategy to achieve good life is pursuing own desires/interest
    \item Adam Smith's ``invisible hands'':
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Free competition and good information creates a moral outcome
        \item Everyone gets to participate equally in the market, bad behavior will be punished
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\k{Utilitarianism}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Foundation is the ``greatest happines principle'':
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item An action is morally right if it results in the greatest amount of good for the greatest amount of people affected by the action
        \item Maximize utility of actions
    \end{itemize}
    \item Egoism focuses on individuals, utilitarianism focuses on collective welfare
    \item Close to cost-benefit analysis (pleasure/pain, happiness/unhappiness)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Problems with utilitarianism}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Subjectivity on assessing gains and pains of an action
    \item Quantification and comparability issue (eg. can losing a good contract be compared with child labour?)
    \item Distribution of utility $\to$ interests of minorities are overlooked (eg. children)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Refined theories}
\subsubsection{Act utilitarianism}
Looks at a single action and bases moral judgement on its consequences
for everyone affected, choosing the action that produces the greatest net benefit.

\pph{Example}
A factory manager compares running an extra weekend shift to produce urgently
needed hospital equipment with keeping the factory closed. If the benefits to
patients and the extra income for workers outweigh the workers' fatigue and the
additional costs, act utilitarianism supports the extra shift in this particular
situation.

\subsubsection{Rule utilitarianism}
Looks at general rules and judges actions by whether they follow rules whose
widespread adoption would produce the greatest net benefit for society in the long run.

\pph{Example}
A company follows the rule ``Give customers truthful information about products'',
even when a misleading advertisement could increase sales in a particular case.
Rule utilitarianism supports this rule because its widespread adoption helps
customers make informed choices, prevents harm, and builds long-term trust in markets.

\subsection{\k{Ethics of duties (Immanuel Kant's approach)}}
Ethics of duties is defined by a set of rationally derived moral rules.

Categorical imperative constitutes a test whether an action is ethical:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Maxim \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, labelindent=0pt, align=left]
    \item \textbf{Consistency}\\
    $\to$ An action is only right if the rule that guides certain ehavior is followed by everyone\\
    Example: Do not make false promises to customers; if everyone did, promises would lose meaning.
    \item \textbf{Human dignity}\\
    $\to$ An action is only right, when respect of humans as autonomous, rational actors is preserved\\
    Example: Give workers full information about risks so they can freely decide whether to accept a task.
    \item \textbf{Universality}\\
    $\to$ Rules should be acceptable by everyone\\
    $\to$ Tries to overcome the risk of subjectivity\\
    Example: Use hiring criteria you could justify to every applicant, including those rejected.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Example 1: A company hides a product defect to increase sales.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistency:} Fails; universal deception would undermine trust in product claims.
    \item \textbf{Human dignity:} Fails; customers cannot make an informed choice.
    \item \textbf{Universality:} Fails; deceived customers could not reasonably accept this rule.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example 2: A company discloses a product defect and offers a free repair or refund.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistency:} Passes; all companies could follow this rule without contradiction.
    \item \textbf{Human dignity:} Passes; customers receive honest information and a free choice.
    \item \textbf{Universality:} Passes; fair remedies for defective products are reasonably acceptable to all.
\end{itemize}

\pph{Example}
You are the product manager of a company manufacturing small plastic toys and
got in contact with a Thai manufacturer, which could produce your toys at a third
of the current cost

During a visit at the manufacturer you recognize that there is no workshop.
Instead at 6am 30 men line up at the company's gate and take the toy
components to their homes

You demand to visit one of their homes and discover that children (5--14 years
old) assemble the toys together with their parents, while the grandmother is
preparing the food in a room next door

The company ensures you that this a well-established practice in Thailand and
would guarantee a high quality standard

\pph{Questions}
\textbf{Consistency:} Does every Swiss company operating globally use child labour?

\textbf{Human dignity:} Have the children been regarded and respected as autonomous rational actors?

\textbf{Universality:} Has everyone identified as an autonomous rational actor find this practice acceptable?

\subsubsection{Problem with `Ethics of duties'}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Undervaluing outcomes $\to$ the theory does not assess outcomes of the actions (non-consequentialist theory)
    \item Complexity
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Kant's categorical imperative requires high level of abstraction
        \item Difficult to apply in each and every case
    \end{itemize}
    \item Optimism $\to$ theory is quite optimistic (view of humans as rational actor)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\k{Ethics of rights and justice}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Based on human rights
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Basic, inalienable entitlements that are inherent to all human beings, without exception
    \end{itemize}
    \item Rights also result in duties of other actors to respect them
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Similar to Kant's approach
        \item However, ``Ethics of rights and justice'' does not rely on determining rights/duties through the categorical imperative
        \item Rights/duties are based on consensus about the nature of human dignity
    \end{itemize}
    \item Human rights is one of the most fundamental theoretical approaches of modern CE (UN's Universal Declaration of Human Rights)
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item More than 7000 corporations have signed the UN Global Compact
        \item Increasing number of businesses develop their own human rights policies (``Ruggie Principles'')
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\k{The problem of justice}}
Justice = simultaneous fair treatment of individuals in a given situation
with the results that everybody gets what they deserve

Theories of justice see fairness in two ways and justice would
ideally seek to achieve both types of fairness:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fair procedure}\\
    $\to$ Fairness is judged baed on the transparency of the processes by which decisions are made (procedural justice)
    \item \textbf{Fair outcomes}\\
    $\to$ Fairness is judged whether consequences are distributed in a just manner based on some underlying principles as need or merit (distributive justice)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Limits of traditional theories}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Too abstract $\to$ managers are unlikely to apply abstract principles
    \item Too reductionist\\
    $\to$ each theory tends to focus on one aspect of morality
    $\to$ why choose consequence, duties, rights when when all are important
    \item Too rational $\to$ theories don't consider moral feelings and emotions (humans are rational actor)
    \item Too imperialistic $\to$ why assume that ethical theories from the West are suitable for businesses else in the world
\end{itemize}

\pph{\k{Cognitive moral development (CMD)}}
Division of moral development in three levels (as people mature through childhood into adulthood)
\begin{enumerate}[label={\textbf{Level \arabic*:}}, leftmargin=*, labelindent=0pt, align=left]
    \item \textbf{Pre-conventional}\\
        Individual exhibits a concern with self-interest and externals rewards/punishment (relativistic)
    \item \textbf{Conventional}\\
        Individual does what is expected of them by others (relativistic)
    \item \textbf{Post-conventional}\\
        Individual is developing autonomous decision-making based on principles of rights and justice (absolute)
\end{enumerate}

\k{\textbf{Personal values}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Individual beliefs about desirable behaviors and goals that are stable over time and which influence decision-making
    \item ``Personal value is an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct''
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
        \item Values persist over time
        \item Values influence behavior
        \item Values are concerned with individual and/or collective well-being
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Personal integrity}\\
An individual's adherence to a consistent set of moral principles, even when
acting on those principles is difficult or involves personal costs.

\textbf{Whistleblowing:} An intentional act by employees to expose
perceived ethical or legal violations by their organization, internally or externally.
\begin{itemize}[label=$\to$]
    \item Can express personal integrity when employees act on their moral
    principles despite pressure to remain silent.
    \item Organizational pressure can suppress such action through fear of
    job loss, blacklisting or social exclusion.
    \item Illustrates the gap between making a moral judgement and actually
    acting on it.
\end{itemize}
